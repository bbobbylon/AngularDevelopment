For a decade, every component lived inside an NgModule; standalone components made the wrapper optional, then made its absence the default. You will still read NgModule code in every mature codebase, migrate it a file at a time, and need to explain why its rules produced the errors they did — which is exactly what its compilation-scope model turns out to be.

The problem

Why does the exact same component compile in one module and not another?

Here is the sentence that explains almost every NgModule error ever filed on Stack Overflow: a component is not visible just because it exists. It has to be reachable from wherever you are trying to use it, and "reachable" was never automatic — it was a set of rules four arrays enforced, and most people learned the rules from the error messages rather than from anyone explaining them first.

Once you accept that visibility is a rule rather than a given, three real questions fall out of it:

  • What decides whether a template can see a component at all? (declarations and exports)
  • Why does a service seem to ignore that rule completely? (the asymmetry)
  • What does standalone drop when it removes the module — and what silently breaks? (the migration)

The mental model

An NgModule is a clubhouse with a guest list — wired to the same street electricity as everyone else.

Picture a clubhouse. The members inside can use the room freely — that's a declaration. At the door there's a guest list: names the clubhouse is willing to vouch for to other clubhouses. That list is the export — and it is short on purpose, because most members are not meant to wander into a different clubhouse's meeting. When another clubhouse imports yours, its members get access to exactly the names on your list. Nobody else.

Now picture the electricity. Every clubhouse on the street is wired into the same grid the moment it's built. The grid does not check the guest list. A clubhouse that never invited anyone still draws power from the same source as the one next door, and the one after that. That's a provider: it has nothing to do with who is or isn't on anyone's door.

This is the single fact that makes NgModules confusing: one wall, two completely different rules. The guest list controls who can walk in and use a component's template. It has zero say over who can plug into a shared service.

Declarations are the room. Exports are the guest list. Imports mean you were invited. Providers are the electricity — wired to the whole street the instant the module loads, guest list or not.

The mechanism

What does “in scope” actually mean to the compiler?

Four arrays, and only the first three ever talk to each other. Here is a module declaring, importing, exporting and providing all at once — the whole vocabulary in one block.

This module's own components, directives and pipes. Private to it unless exports says otherwise. Other modules' exports, pulled into this one. Never their private declarations. The subset of declarations (or of an import) this module shares. The whole public API. Services registered with DI. Ignores exports completely — an eagerly loaded module's providers reach the whole app.
Providers ignore exports entirely. An eagerly loaded module's providers array merges into the app-wide injector the instant the module loads — whether you exported a single thing or nothing at all. It is the one line in the metadata that doesn't play by the other three's rules, and it's exactly what the mental model above is trying to warn you about.

The migration gotcha

What happens when the free ride ends?

A component's template can use a directive it never imported, so long as some ancestor module's exports chain reaches it. Nobody notices this is happening, because it never had to be written down — until standalone makes writing it down the whole point.

If a component's template only worked because some ancestor module re-exported the directive it needed, standalone makes it say so directly. Add the import yourself, or the directive disappears with no build error to point at.

History

Why did forRoot and forChild exist in the first place?

A module imported by five lazy features would register its providers five times — five instances of a "singleton". forRoot() was the workaround: a plain static method returning module-plus-providers for the one place that should register them.

You'll still meet forRoot() in older third-party libraries that haven't shipped a provide*() function yet — recognizing the pattern still matters even after you've stopped writing it yourself.

The new world

How does standalone split the same four jobs?

  • Template deps live on the component — its imports array names exactly what its template uses (components, directives, pipes, or whole NgModules).
  • standalone is the default — modern components don't write standalone: true; NgModule-declared components are the ones that need explicit marking (standalone: false).
  • Providers centralize — app.config.ts for the app, route providers for feature scope, component providers for per-instance.

The trap that never throws

Your build is clean. Is your auth header still being sent?

AppModule used to register AuthInterceptor the old way — a class, wired in through the HTTP_INTERCEPTORS multi-token. The schematic deletes AppModule and hands you provideHttpClient() in its place. It compiles. Nothing throws.

Mixing worlds

Can NgModules and standalone components coexist mid-migration?

Both directions work, so migrate leaf-first without a big bang: convert leaf components to standalone (NgModules can still import them), walk up the tree, delete each module when its last declaration leaves, and finish by swapping platformBrowserDynamic().bootstrapModule(AppModule) for bootstrapApplication(App, appConfig).

Questions from the back row

Before you go

Five things to remember.

  • A component isn't visible just for existing. Declarations are private; exports is the only door out, and imports is how another module walks through it.
  • Providers ignore all of that. An eagerly loaded module's providers merge into the app-wide injector whether or not anything was ever exported — the asymmetry behind years of confusion.
  • forRoot()/forChild() existed purely to fake provider scoping for lazy features; provide*() functions made the whole dance unnecessary.
  • A component that "just worked" under NgModules may have been living off an ancestor module's re-exports. Standalone removes that free ride — say what you use, on the component itself, or it silently stops rendering.
  • The scariest migration bugs are the ones that compile clean and fail quietly — a dropped HTTP_INTERCEPTORS registration being the textbook case. Review provider-only modules by hand; the schematic can't know what they were for.
