An Observable is a stream you can watch. A Subject is a stream you can watch and speak into — it is an Observable and an Observer at the same time, which is why you can call next() on one. That single extra ability is what lets imperative code ("the user clicked", "the socket said something") get into a reactive pipeline at all, and it is what turns one private run into a broadcast everyone shares. There are four flavours, and they differ in exactly one respect: what a subscriber is told when it arrives late.

The problem

You cannot speak into an Observable.

Everything in the previous two lessons flowed one way. You described a producer — interval(), http.get(), fromEvent() — and then you subscribed and watched it run. At no point were you allowed to reach in from the side and say "and now, a 7". There is no next() on an Observable. There is nowhere to put one.

That is a problem the moment your values come from something the observable does not own. A button in one component. A message off a WebSocket. A toast that any of forty places might want to raise. You have a value and an audience, and the tool you know about only works when the producer and the subscription are the same piece of code.

And there is a second, quieter problem sitting next to it:

  • An Observable is cold — the producer is built inside the subscribe call.
  • So two subscribers do not share a run. They get two runs.
  • Which means two timers, two WebSocket connections, or two HTTP requests.
Commit to this before you scroll. A stream emits 1, then 2, then 3. Only now does a second subscriber arrive. What should it be told? Write down an answer — and then notice that you cannot, because "it depends" is the honest response and the thing it depends on is the whole of this lesson. There are four different right answers below, and a live bench that will show you all four at once.

The mental model

An Observable is a recipe. A Subject is a radio station.

Hand two people the same recipe and you get two dinners. The recipe is not the food; it is instructions for making food, and it runs again from the top for every person who picks it up. That is a cold Observable, exactly: the code that produces values sits inside the subscribe function, so subscribing is what runs it, and every subscriber runs their own copy.

A radio station is the other thing entirely. There is one broadcast, going out now, whether or not anyone is listening. Everybody tuned in hears the same words at the same moment — that is multicast — and if you switch the radio on halfway through the news, the first half is simply gone. That is hot. A Subject is the station: you hold the microphone, you call next(), and everyone currently tuned in hears it.

Which leaves precisely one interesting question, and it is the question the four flavours exist to answer differently. Somebody tunes in late. What do you tell them?

Nothing, Now, Rewind, Finale. That is the four flavours in the order you will be asked about them. Subject tells a latecomer nothing. BehaviorSubject tells it what is true now. ReplaySubject rewinds the last N. AsyncSubject says nothing until the finale, then hands over one value.

The mechanism

A Subject is an array and a for-loop.

This is one of the rare places in Angular where the real implementation is small enough to just read. Strip the scheduling, the operator plumbing and the error handling out of RxJS's Subject and what is left is a list of callbacks and a loop over it. Cold and hot

Cold means everybody gets a private run.

"Cold" and "hot" sound like properties of the data. They are not. They are a statement about where the producer lives. If the thing that makes the values is created inside the subscribe function, every subscription builds a new one, and the stream is cold. If the producer already exists and the subscribe function only adds you to a list, the stream is hot.

So how do you get from the first picture to the second? You put a Subject in the middle. It subscribes to the cold source once, and then everybody subscribes to it. This is not a workaround — it is the mechanism RxJS itself uses, and once you have seen the wiring by hand, share() stops looking like magic.

The four flavours

Four types. One question.

All four are Subjects: all four multicast, all four take a next(), all four die on complete(). The only thing they disagree about is what happens in the instant a new subscriber arrives — which, when you think about it, is the only decision there is to make.

No memory whatsoever. You hear values emitted strictly after you subscribed. The right default for events: a click that already happened is not news. Requires a seed value in the constructor, so there is always one to hand over. Has a synchronous .value getter. The classic — and now usually superseded — way to hold state. Keeps a buffer and replays it on subscribe. Takes an optional time window too, so ReplaySubject(5, 30_000) means "the last five, from the last 30 seconds". Silent until complete(), then delivers exactly one value — the last one — to everyone, past and future. Rare in app code; it is the shape of "a promise, done the RxJS way".

The trap

complete() and error() are one-way doors.

A Subject has exactly one thing it can never take back. complete() and error() both empty the observer list, both set a stopped flag, and both store the ending so that every subscriber arriving afterwards can be handed it immediately. There is no way back. Not a method, not a flag, not a trick — you replace the subject or you live without it.

The cruel part is how quiet the failure is. Calling next() on a terminated Subject does not throw, does not warn and does not queue. It returns.

Do this in order and watch the log: add a listener, publish twice, press error(), then publish twice more. Those last two lines appear in the log with nothing underneath them. Then add a second listener and watch it get handed the error it was never present for.

If you are ever debugging "my event definitely fired and nothing happened", check whether something completed the subject first.

The discipline

Never hand out the microphone.

A public Subject is a public next(), a public complete() and a public error(). Every file in the application can now write to your state, and — much worse — any one of them can terminate a stream the whole app depends on, from a line of code nobody will think to look at.

The shape to memorise: private readonly x = new Subject<T>(), then readonly x$ = this.x.asObservable(), then one public method per thing that can happen. If you find yourself typing public in front of a Subject, that is the moment to stop.

The leak

A Subject never ends on its own.

An http.get() completes after one response, so a subscription to it cleans itself up and forgetting to unsubscribe costs you nothing. A Subject modelling an ongoing feed has no such courtesy: it is infinite by design. Subscribe in a component, navigate away, and your callback is still in that array — holding a reference to a component that no longer exists, and still running every time somebody calls next().

There are three tools, and picking between them is easy. Use the async pipe when the value goes straight into a template. Use toSignal() when it goes into a signal. Use takeUntilDestroyed() when you are subscribing for a genuine side effect.

Do not "clean up" by completing somebody else's subject. Calling complete() in your ngOnDestroy on a providedIn: 'root' subject does not unsubscribe you — it kills the stream for the entire application, permanently, and the symptom shows up in a completely unrelated component.

In Angular 21

Most BehaviorSubjects want to be signals.

For about a decade, "hold state in a BehaviorSubject, expose it with asObservable(), read it with the async pipe" was simply the answer. It is now the second-best answer for the most common case, because a signal offers the identical contract — always has a current value, always readable synchronously — with no subscription, no teardown, and change detection that understands it natively.

Reading it anywhere means count(). Nothing to unsubscribe, and a computed() away from derived state.

So when is a Subject still the right tool? Whenever the thing you are modelling is not a value but an occurrence — and especially whenever you need time semantics. A signal has no vocabulary for "wait 300 milliseconds", "only if nothing newer arrives", "cancel the previous one". RxJS is built out of those words, and that is why this next component is still the correct shape in a signals-first application.

What you have Reach for Because
The current user, a filter, an open/closed flag signal() "What is it right now?" has an answer, so it is state

State is a signal. Events are a Subject. If "what is it right now?" has a sensible answer, you want a signal. If the only sensible question is "what just happened?", you want a Subject — and if the answer involves the word when, you definitely do.

Questions from the back row

Before you go

Six things to remember.

  • A Subject is both an Observable and an Observer. That is not trivia — it is why you can call next() on it, and why you can pass it straight to coldSource$.subscribe(subject).
  • Inside, it is a list of callbacks and a for-loop. That is the whole of multicasting, and it is why subscribing to a Subject starts nothing while subscribing to a cold Observable runs a producer.
  • Nothing, Now, Rewind, Finale. Subject, BehaviorSubject, ReplaySubject(n), AsyncSubject — four answers to the one question of what a latecomer is told.
  • complete() and error() are permanent. The observer list is emptied, later next() calls are silently dropped, and newcomers are handed the stored ending. And a completed BehaviorSubject gives a newcomer nothing — where a ReplaySubject still replays.
  • Keep the subject private and publish asObservable(). It returns a real object without next() on it, so this is a fence rather than a convention. And a Subject never completes on its own, so always tear the subscription down — async pipe, toSignal() or takeUntilDestroyed().
  • State is a signal; events are a Subject. In a modern Angular app most BehaviorSubjects are legacy state, but a debounced, cancelled, retried stream of things that happened is exactly what RxJS is for.
