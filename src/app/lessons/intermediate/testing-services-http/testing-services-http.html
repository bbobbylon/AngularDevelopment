Services are the easiest things to test, because they're just classes. HTTP calls need a little more machinery, but Angular's HttpTestingController hands you full, synchronous control over every request that goes out — no network, no timers, no async timing to get wrong. This page proves both halves, then adds interceptors, RxJS, and the shape a test suite should have when someone else has to read it.

The problem

This test has been green for eight months. It has never once run.

Picture a test called fetches a user. It has passed on every commit for as long as anyone can remember — local runs, CI, all of it. Nobody has ever seen it fail. Here's the part that should worry you: it has never once evaluated the assertion written inside it, on any of those runs.

That isn't a hypothetical. It's the single easiest mistake to make anywhere in this area of testing, and by the end of this page you'll be able to spot it on sight. For now, just hold the question: how does a test manage to stay green without the check inside it ever actually running?

Keep an answer in your head, even a vague one. The rest of this page is Angular's answer, in five parts: what a service test looks like with nothing faked at all, what HttpTestingController actually controls, how interceptors sit inside that control, how far RxJS testing needs to go, and the shape that keeps all of it readable a year from now. There's a real, working version of that broken test waiting partway down this page — and a chance to prove, for yourself, exactly why it never had to run.

The mental model

Angular's HTTP testing backend has no kitchen behind it. You are the kitchen.

Here's the mental model that makes the rest of this page fall into place. Picture your service as a waiter: it writes an order — GET /api/users/1 — clips the ticket to the rail at the pass, and waits. In a real app, a kitchen on the other side cooks something and hands back a plate. In a test, provideHttpClientTesting() fires that kitchen. There's no cook back there any more. You are the only thing that can take a ticket off the rail and send a plate back out.

Everything else follows from that one substitution. expectOne is you reading a ticket off the rail — and complaining if there are two tickets, or none. flush is you handing back a plate, with whatever you decide is on it. And because you are the kitchen, nothing at all happens until you act: no network, no timer, no waiting. Which is exactly why these tests run synchronously, and why http.verify() at the end — a glance at the rail to check no ticket is still hanging there — is not optional politeness. It's the only thing that notices an order you never cooked.

Expect the ticket, flush the plate, verify the rail. Every HTTP test is those three moves, in that order. Skip the third and a test that asserted nothing at all still passes.

Where testing is easiest

A service with no dependencies is just a class.

Before HTTP enters the picture at all: if a service has no injected dependencies, testing it needs no Angular machinery whatsoever. new it, call its public methods, and read back what comes out — the fastest test this app can write, and worth reaching for whenever it's honestly available.

RxJS & marbles

Most of the time you don't need marbles. Here's the one time you do.

Marble testing describes an Observable sequence as an ASCII string and compares it, frame for frame, against what your operators actually produced. It's precise, and it's overkill for almost everything — reach for it only when the timing itself is the behaviour under test, like a debounce or a race between two streams.

Patterns that survive a refactor

Match the setup to what the service actually needs — never more.

Reach for the lightest setup that's still honest about what the service does
Scenario Pattern
Pure service (signals only) Plain new Service() — fastest
Service with inject() dependencies TestBed.configureTestingModule with providers
Service makes HTTP calls provideHttpClientTesting + HttpTestingController
Service has a timer/polling loop fakeAsync + tick/flush to control time
Service depends on another service Inject a mock/spy — useValue + vi.fn()
Test an interceptor Register with withInterceptors([...]) + HttpTestingController
Service uses localStorage Set/assert directly in beforeEach/afterEach
What not to test, and why
Thing Why not
Private methods, directly Test through the public API — a private method is not a contract, and internals can change freely
Angular internals (that HttpClient calls the right URL) Angular already tests that — you test your own logic built around it
Every getter/setter, independently Test behaviours, not structure: "cart total updates when an item is added"

Questions from the back row

Before you go

Six things to remember.

  • Pure services: new Service() — no TestBed, the fastest test there is.
  • HTTP: provideHttpClientTesting() + HttpTestingController give synchronous control — no network, no timers, no waiting.
  • expectOne + flush/error assert the request and decide what comes back; http.verify() in afterEach is what catches an unhandled request, or a callback that never ran.
  • Provider order is not cosmetic: provideHttpClientTesting() must come after provideHttpClient() in the same array, or the real backend wins and every request in the suite goes out for real.
  • Test interceptors through the real HttpClient, registered with withInterceptors([...]) — the chain, not the function in isolation, is the thing actually under test.
  • Mock collaborators with useValue + vi.fn(); reach for vi.spyOn when you want the real service with one method faked. Marble testing earns its keep only when timing itself is the behaviour under test.
