A structural directive changes the shape of the DOM — it adds and removes whole template blocks. It's the exact mechanism behind @if, @for and the legacy *ngIf/*ngFor. The trick it's built on: a directive can inject the template it's attached to (TemplateRef) and a place to render copies of it (ViewContainerRef), then stamp or clear that template on demand.

Flip [hidden] on a component and the pixels vanish — right on schedule. The subscription it opened in ngOnInit? Still delivering values. The timer? Still ticking. Nothing was ever told to stop.

[hidden] only ever toggles a CSS property on the host element. The component instance stays mounted, its constructor never runs again because it was never destroyed, and its ngOnDestroy — the one place cleanup was supposed to happen — never fires.

A structural directive removes the node from the DOM entirely. The component is genuinely destroyed: ngOnDestroy fires, its subscriptions are torn down with it, and nothing about it survives until the condition flips back and a fresh instance is built.

The mental model, in full

Picture a developer holding blueprints, standing on an empty lot.

A TemplateRef is a set of architectural drawings. It is a real, complete description of a building — every wall, every wire — and absolutely nobody lives in it. A ViewContainerRef is an empty lot with a surveyor's stake in the ground. Your structural directive is the developer holding both, and createEmbeddedView is the moment it decides to actually build.

Three things fall straight out of that picture. You can build the same drawings on the lot five times, which is exactly what *appRepeat does below. vcr.clear() is demolition rather than boarding up the windows — the components inside are destroyed and their subscriptions torn down, which is the real difference between a structural directive and [hidden]. And the stake stays in the ground whether or not anything is built on it, which is that <!--container--> comment you keep seeing in DevTools.

TemplateRef is the blueprint; ViewContainerRef is the lot. The * is just the paperwork that turns your markup into drawings instead of a house.

Pulling it together

Wrong way vs right way.

Traps this pattern invites
Trap Fix
Two structural directives on one element (*ngIf + *ngFor) Nest them, or use @if inside @for
Re-creating the view on every setter call without checking state Track a rendered flag (or diff the input) before stamping/clearing
Rebuilding the whole list each change (like our simple *appRepeat) Real lists should diff & reuse views — that's what @for's track does
Untyped context vars Add ngTemplateContextGuard
Modern Angular favours the built-in @if/@for blocks for everyday conditionals and lists — they're faster and need no import. Reach for a custom structural directive when you have a reusable rendering pattern: role-based access (*appHasRole), a permission gate, or custom repetition with bespoke context.

Questions from the back row

Before you go

Things to remember.

  • The * prefix desugars to an <ng-template> wrapper — that's why only one per element, and why nesting (not stacking) is how you combine two.
  • Inject TemplateRef (the blueprint) and ViewContainerRef (where to stamp it).
  • createEmbeddedView(tpl, context) renders; vcr.clear() removes and destroys.
  • The context's $implicit feeds bare let; named keys feed let x = key.
  • Add ngTemplateContextGuard to type-check let variables; prefer @if/@for for the common cases and save a custom directive for a reusable decision.
